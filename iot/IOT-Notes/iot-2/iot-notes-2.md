## Sensing

- A sensor detects (senses) changes in the ambient conditions or in the state of another device or a system, and forwards or processes this information in a certain manner [1].
- A sensor is *“a device which detects or measures a physical property and records, indicates, or otherwise responds to it”* [2] — Oxford Dictionary.

**References**

1. [BusinessDictionary – Sensor](http://www.businessdictionary.com/definition/sensor.html)  
2. [Oxford Dictionary – Sensor](https://en.oxforddictionaries.com/definition/sensor)

---

## Sensors

- Sensors perform input functions by sensing or feeling the physical changes in the characteristics of a system in response to stimuli.
- Example: heat is converted to electrical signals in a temperature sensor, or atmospheric pressure is converted to electrical signals in a barometer.

---

## Transducers

- Transducers convert (transduce) energy of one kind into another.
- Example: in a sound system, a microphone (input device) converts sound waves into electrical signals for an amplifier to amplify, and a loudspeaker (output device) converts these electrical signals back into sound waves.

---

## Sensor Features

A good sensor typically:

- Is only sensitive to the measured property (e.g., a temperature sensor senses the ambient temperature of a room).
- Is insensitive to other properties likely to be encountered in its application (e.g., a temperature sensor does not bother about light or pressure while sensing the temperature).
- Does not influence the measured property (e.g., measuring the temperature does not significantly reduce or increase the temperature).

---

## Sensor Resolution

- The sensor resolution (or measurement resolution) is the smallest change that can be detected in the quantity that is being measured.
- The resolution of a sensor with a digital output is usually the numerical resolution of the digital output.
- Higher resolution generally gives more precise measurements.
- A sensor’s accuracy does **not** depend solely on its resolution.

---

## Sensor Classes

### Based on Output Type

- **Analog sensors**: produce a continuous output signal or voltage that is generally proportional to the quantity being measured.
- **Digital sensors**: produce discrete digital output signals or voltages that are a digital representation of the quantity being measured.

### Based on Data Type

- **Scalar sensors**: output is proportional only to the magnitude of the quantity being measured.
- **Vector sensors**: output depends on the magnitude, direction, and orientation of the quantity being measured.

---

## Digital Sensors

- Digital sensors produce a binary output signal in the form of logic “1” or logic “0” (“ON” or “OFF”).
- Digital signals only produce discrete (non‑continuous) values.
- Output may be:
  - A single bit (serial transmission), or
  - A group of bits combined to produce a single byte (parallel transmission).

---

## Analog Sensors

- Analog sensors produce a continuous output signal or voltage that is generally proportional to the quantity being measured.
- Physical quantities such as temperature, speed, pressure, displacement, and strain are all analog quantities as they tend to be continuous in nature.
- Example: the temperature of a liquid can be measured using a thermometer or thermocouple (e.g., in geysers), which continuously responds to temperature changes as the liquid is heated up or cooled down.

---

## Scalar Sensors

- Scalar sensors produce an output signal or voltage that is generally proportional to the magnitude of the quantity being measured.
- Physical quantities such as temperature, color, pressure, and strain are all scalar quantities, as only their magnitude is sufficient to convey the information.
- Example: the temperature of a room can be measured using a thermometer or thermocouple, which responds to temperature changes irrespective of the orientation of the sensor or its direction.

---

## Vector Sensors

- Vector sensors produce an output signal or voltage that is generally proportional to the magnitude, direction, and orientation of the quantity being measured.
- Physical quantities such as sound, image, velocity, acceleration, and orientation are all vector quantities, as only their magnitude is not sufficient to convey the complete information.
- Example: the acceleration of a body can be measured using an accelerometer, which gives the components of acceleration of the body with respect to the \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) coordinate axes.

---

## Sensor Types (Examples)

- **Light sensors**: photodiode, photoresistor (LDR).
- **Temperature sensors**: thermocouple, thermistor.
- **Strain/pressure sensors**: strain gauge, pressure switch.
- **Position sensors**: potentiometer, encoders.
- **Optical sensors**: reflective / Doppler‑effect opto‑coupler sensor.
- **Sound sensors**: carbon microphone.
- **Chemical sensors**: liquid/gaseous chemical sensors.

## Hysteresis Error

- A hysteresis error causes the sensor output value to vary depending on the sensor’s previous input values.
- If a sensor's output is different for the same input value depending on whether that value was reached by increasing or decreasing the input, then the sensor has a hysteresis error.
- **The present reading depends on the past input values.**
- Hysteresis is common in many analog sensors, magnetic sensors, and in devices involving heating of metal strips.
